Historical roots of cowardice as masculine

Harry Potter is the quintessential toxic masculine male that’s still attractive to women. He is legitimately ruggedly individual, but also has some traditionally toxic masculine flaws.

Harry is quick to anger and sometimes accidentally uses magic angrily and endangers the lives of his extended family. He doesn’t disagree with Hermione saying that she could never be as brave as him. He won’t date Ginny without her brother Ron’s approval. He only has real relationships with male teachers. He regularly takes risks that defy common sense and force Hermione to mother him (emotional labor). He isn’t as good a student as Hermione or able to shield his mind like Snape, but gets by on the privilege of a mother that broke a curse, a family that provides immense wealth and support, and genes that make him instantly good at Quidditch.

So when did we go from Harry to oxymoronically describing a man not attractive to women as masculine? Masculine by definition means “having qualities or an appearance traditionally associated with men or boys” — how could the traditional qualities of men or boys be antithetical to attracting a mate?

One possibility is the myth that women traditionally had no say in who they married. In that mythical world winning a wife would be more about appealing to a father or brother.

However in the real world of “Normal Women” by Phillipa Gregory:

Gregory argues that because ordinary women did not have massive estates, political alliances, or dowries at stake, their marriages were often based on personal choice, mutual labor needs, or affection rather than familial strategy.

Also included in Gregory’s book is the story of how women hunted the forests to provide for their families, equally ran many of the businesses, and even solely started and ran many businesses.
