Finally got Abundance from the library as I refuse to fund this nonsense (hopefully I’m not causing the library to buy another license).

Abundance purports to be a vision of the future. In order for that to be true it has to solve three potentially catastrophic problems:

  • climate change
  • baby bust (there is nothing wrong with decreasing population but sometime in the future it has to stabilize)
  • wealth inequality (I believe wealth inequality causes the rise of fascism but even if not you again need to stabilize in the future or all the world’s resources will be in a few hands — this happens sooner than you think because of a snowball effect see Capital in the Twenty-First Century)

Below I show that Abundance does a poor job solving anything, but particularly wealth inequality. Laying out a vision of the future where humanity basically fails is anti-intellectual at best and oligarchy inspired corrupt ideology at worst.

Here on page seven Abundance says:

In 1950, the median home price was 2.2 times the average annual income; by 2020, it was 6 times the average annual income.

In the 1950s poverty was double what it is now (says Google though that is hard to measure), they didn’t have most appliances or large homes, and overall it was a time of extreme scarcity of consumption compared to now:

[Side note: If we are consuming more now, isn’t that a win for neoliberalism? No because 1) anything that leads to wealth inequality and fascism isn’t sustainable and 2) heavy neoliberalism started in the 1980s.]

Then when you follow the footnote link for that sentence it is all about using better regulations to lower housing costs:

Improving renter protections, expanding social housing and more tightly regulating the mortgage market would slow down housing appreciation. Cutting down on short-term rentals and vacation homes also has a dramatic impact on housing affordability. In a recent study, MIT, UCLA, and USC found that for every 10% growth in Airbnb listings, a zip code’s average rent increased by 0.4%.
Another solution would be to limit foreign investment and speculation. When Vancouver passed a 15% tax on all sales to foreign home buyers, the price of single family property dropped 20% before rebounding, giving housing appreciation a short-term respite.
A more dramatic intervention would be to reverse the trend of corporations getting into the housing market and reintroduce public land ownership. From 2013 to 2015, corporations purchased almost $2 trillion worth of land and buildings in the world’s top 100 cities.

From page 32:

Except that:

Not exclusionary policies were implemented leading to the wealth inequality of bankers / software engineers. Where I live in Silicon Valley it was fruit orchards before the engineers came.
  • Policy levers are to blame for inequality but they are the same neoliberal, mostly federal, policies we all knew about throughout.
  • The everyone move to the cities and be upwardly mobile bullshit has already been tried “The Seoul Metropolitan Area, which includes Seoul, Incheon, and Gyeonggi Province, is home to more than half (50.7%) of the country’s total population.”

[Side note: One might argue that moving to the cities is working in that it is better than the rural life. However 1) Not better enough for South Korea to avoid a near miss with fascism and 2) Not better enough to stem the rising wealth inequality shown here.]

But fine since Klein keeps coming back to it, let’s discuss what happened to California high-speed rail. Yesterday, the Federal Railroad Administration released an over 300 page report:

Most notably, the CHSR Project continues to be marred by countless change orders for almost every contract. Change orders ranged from tens of thousands of dollars to over $100 million. In just over two years, from January 2023 to present, CHSRA has publicly reported approximately $1.6 billion in change orders, suggesting that a substantial portion of all payments to contractors are being made outside the original terms of negotiated contracts. There is no indication that such renegotiations and costly change orders, necessitated by CHSRA’s failure to manage this project effectively, will ever stop.
Simply put, CHSRA is not using State or Federal funding to advance responsibly the CHSR Project. Rather, it is simply perpetuating the same inefficient project management activities with change order after change order. It is no surprise, therefore, why CHSRA has made minimal progress to advance construction. The sheer volume and frequency of these change orders shows waste through an inexcusable combination of poor planning, implementation, or mismanagement of contractors, insufficient legal authority and technical expertise, and other factors.

So that’s the Trump administrations take. However page 47 of the report has a Letter to the Legislature (March 8, 2024) with this sentence:

We cannot emphasize too strongly that lack of action by the Legislature and Governor to identify an adequate and stable source of funding for the project is increasing costs and hindering management’s control of the project.

So Klein says burden of government rules, transportation secretary Duffy says mismanagement, and someone on the project says unstable funding — plenty of finger pointing.

However the take away of “everything-bagel liberalism” from the wreckage of this project is just a giant neoliberal smoke screen anyway. Suppose this project succeeds and software engineers are able to commute to Silicon Valley from far away. Then what?

I mean first of all Silicon Valley already outsources much of its engineering to poor countries all over the world. So it’s unclear why anyone needs to ride a train to accomplish that. In fact this project is kind of the opposite of the dynamic cities that Abundance is advocating and more about getting smaller towns involved.

Second how is California high-speed rail even supposed to help wealth inequality? If the point is that people working on building the train system will have quality jobs then spend away — just make sure the money reaches people that are not already rich.

But the money isn’t reaching poorer people because richer people are preventing that. That’s what happens in general with wealth inequality and why allowing it to reach such monstrous levels is so hard to undo.

[Side note: I don’t want to come off as not wanting the many jobs and cool new and more environmentally friendly transportation that high speed rail creates. It’s just that high speed rail and other public works projects are not a vision of the future — for instance Japan leaned heavily into such programs but it didn’t solve their wealth inequality or economic woes.]
