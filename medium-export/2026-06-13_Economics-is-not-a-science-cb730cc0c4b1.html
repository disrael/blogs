Economics is not a science

It would be nice if political decisions could be made by technocratic mathematical calculation but they can’t.

Let’s take a moment to review why real science works. Real science’s predictive power comes from models that are applied under conditions where they work.

So for instance science has a model that says energy is conserved.

The Law of Conservation of Energy states that the total energy of an isolated system remains constant; energy can neither be created nor destroyed, only transformed from one form to another.

But you have to read the fine print:

The principle of energy conservation applies locally. If you measure energy in a localized, unchanging region (like on Earth), it holds true. However, it fails on a global, universal scale.

So science allows you to make predictions under the right conditions. Economics pretends to be science but for predictive power:

  • Scientific theory
  • Very educated guess
  • Educated guess
  • Biased educated guess — the best economics lives here
  • Very biased educated guess — average economics lives here
  • Mystic bullshit — economics endorsed by the powerful lives here

Even the best economics suffers from status quo bias, natural tendency to prefer the current state of affairs, and hindsight bias, belief that the past easily predicts the future.

The related field of history is much more honest about itself. The International Prize for History does not call itself a Nobel Prize. The Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel created in 1968 by the central bank of Sweden insists on doing that even though human rights lawyer Peter Nobel (great-grandnephew of Alfred Nobel) has complained.

Here’s a speech by Michael S. Barr asking for more financial regulation:

In accounting for the macroeconomic costs and benefits of financial deregulation, there is a trade off between short- and long-term effects. In the near term, deregulation can deliver something akin to a sugar high, in the form of more lending or market activities and higher profits. But this comes at the expense of greater vulnerability and risk for the financial system and the economy in the longer term. This hiding behind science means that, even when talking about some of the largest fraud in history, Michael can only use language like “trade off between short- and long-term effects”.

Michael can’t appeal to our sense of morality because his cosplay as a scientist requires him to purely make predictions about numbers IE macroeconomic costs. Michael cannot say that giving large sums of money to known thieves and looking the other way is just plain wrong, because he is instead invoking an economic law of conservation of regulation and risk.

The problem is that lifetime appointments for Supreme Court justices and 14 year appointments for Federal Reserve members are blatantly wrong. There is simply no precedent for granting political decision makers anywhere near this duration of power.

Across the world, the longest term any politician in a democracy gets is 7 years. Its universally understood that beyond 5 or 6 years accountability is an issue.

Why then have positions in the US government that are deliberately unaccountable? Because they are considered mere stewards of pseudo scientific principles. Take for instance this 2021 economics paper with lead author Lisa Cook.

We built a vibrant middle class in the post-war era because we started to tear down many of the barriers that had kept women of all races and men of color from contributing their full talents. A quarter of the growth in aggregate output from 1960 to 2010 can be explained by improved allocation of talent.

See we don’t have to make political decisions about regulating markets, we simply have to mathematically calculate what will produce the most profit.

Making political decisions requires messy appeals to emotion and working towards a society with a shared sense of values. Making “economic” decisions requires only rationality and logic.

In fact since we are only concerned with profit we need only ask “market contacts” their opinions when we monitor markets as Philip N. Jefferson explains:

As part of our financial stability monitoring work at the Fed, we routinely survey market contacts regarding potential risks to the U.S. financial system. A finding in the most recent survey is likely of particular interest to a conference exploring financial stability in a time of technological change — 30 percent of contacts surveyed cited a turn in the prevailing sentiment toward AI as a salient risk to the U.S. financial system and the global economy. That was up from 9 percent in the spring survey. Participants noted that if such an unwinding were to occur, it could tighten financial conditions and restrain economic activity more broadly. It is not my intention to make a judgement on the likelihood of those, or other scenarios offered in the FSR. Rather, I want to reiterate that I am monitoring the scenarios.
