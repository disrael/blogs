More education is not always the answer

Key aspects of PhD labor conditions in Korea:

  • Manual Labor in Labs: Many doctoral candidates report being used by professors for research farms, machinery operation, and personal tasks. The academic culture often allows for extreme working hours (8 am to 11 pm or midnight) and requires high loyalty to supervisors.
  • Low-Wage Trap: With a record high of nearly 20,000 new doctorates in 2025, the market is over-saturated, leading to high-qualified individuals taking low-wage positions.
  • Shift to Skilled Manual Work: Some reports suggest shifting to technical, high-paying manual trades (e.g., plumbing, construction, electrical work) as a more viable alternative to underpaid academic positions, as these fields face labor shortages.

If it can happen there it can happen in America also. Furthermore it is the sense of zero sum game, and not one’s relative wealth, that is the largest contributing factor to Korean fascism:

This shift toward the far-right among young Koreans is not driven by marginalization or rising inequality. Rather, it is a reaction from privileged youth — those resisting what they perceive as a loss of their advantage. In fact, over the past decade, inequality indicators like the Gini coefficient have improved in Korea. The significance of educational pedigree has also declined. In the past, elite men from top universities in Seoul could expect good jobs without much trouble. Today, they must compete with women in the labor market. While men still earn more on average, women’s income growth has outpaced that of men over the same period. The pace of job creation hasn’t kept up with the level of competition, leading those who were previously better off to feel like their opportunities are shrinking in a “zero-sum game.”

Epstein likens this future to the propagation of ATMs. Back in the day, pundits predicted that bank tellers would be laid off en masse as the machines took over. But the opposite happened. Banks became more efficient, leading to a trend of more banks opening and more tellers being hired. Today, tellers spend less time counting money (an easy task to automate) and more time building relationships with clients (tasks computers can’t do well).

But here’s what actually happened:

Bank teller employment has transitioned from a period of steady growth during the introduction of ATMs (1980s–2000s) to a sharp decline in the 2010s and 2020s, with a projected 13% decline in the U.S. from 2024 to 2034. While automation initially expanded the role by making it cheaper to open new branches, the rise of digital and mobile banking reduced the need for physical branch visits, reversing this trend.

That projected 13% decline does not include the even further automation that AI will make possible so it will be much higher.

As banks invest less in human tellers things can get stressful and unions are sought for the first time.
