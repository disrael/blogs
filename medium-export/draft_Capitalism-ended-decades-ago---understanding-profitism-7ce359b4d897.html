Capitalism ended decades ago — understanding profitism

And no profit didn’t go up because corporations are more efficient — if anything it’s the opposite:

Meanwhile, productivity growth has stalled. From 1948 to 2004, U.S. labor productivity among nonfinancial firms grew by an annual average of 2.5%. Since then its growth has averaged just 1.1%. That’s no coincidence: Bureaucracy is particularly virulent in large companies, which have come to dominate the U.S. economy. More than a third of the U.S. labor force now works in firms with more than 5,000 employees — where those on the front lines are buried under eight levels of management, on average.

Some time around 2008, as the government bailed out banks, the focus of our economy shifted from productivity to gaming the system. Corporations stopped competing and focused their efforts on achieving near monopoly market dominance:

In the 1970s, an ideological revolution that celebrated bigness and corporate scale swept through the fields of law and economics. One result was a radical change in how the antitrust laws are interpreted, such that their long-established goals of protecting competition and decentralizing power were abandoned. Instead, antitrust enforcement was reconfigured to favor consolidation on the theory that big business delivered greater efficiencies.

Capitalism is “an economic and political system in which a country’s trade and industry are controlled by private owners for profit.” However as corporations got larger and larger the “controlled by private owners” was simply no longer true as no one controls these entities.

Approximately 52.20% of the company’s stock is owned by Institutional Investors, 3.98% is owned by Insiders and 43.83% is owned by Public Companies and Individual Investors.

There is no “private” ownership — it’s big public bureaucracies owned by giant less public bureaucracies and “controlled” by no one.

BlackRock is not owned by a single individual or company. Instead, its shares are owned by a large number of individual and institutional investors. The biggest institutional shareholders such as The Vanguard Group and State Street are merely custodians of the stock for their clients.

In such a system innovation does not increase profit, it just means shifting investments from the old to the new. There is a cost and hassle associated with handling the mostly unwanted innovation and so arguably innovation decreases profit.

Nor is the efficient management of existing holdings really a goal either as efficiency might spur mostly unwanted innovation and also means hiring fewer employees which is politically less stable.

What is being maximized is purely the corporate profit margin so that those profits can be shared throughout all of the giant bureaucracy.

So our current system’s definition is more like an economic and political system in which a country’s trade and industry are controlled by semi public bureaucracies for profit.

Max Weber, a German scientist, defines bureaucracy as a highly structured, formalized, and also an impersonal organization. He also instituted the belief that an organization must have a defined hierarchical structure and clear rules, regulations, and lines of authority which govern it.

Nope then that’s not going to work. A bureaucracy owning another bureaucracy does not in fact have a defined hierarchical structure. It might be closer to a permissionless corporation the higher one goes up.

So controlled by “semi public bureaucracies” is misleading. What about “an economic and political system in which a country’s trade and industry are maximized for stability of profit without ethical consideration.” and we can call it profitism.

The difference between capitalism and profitism is that in profitism the human element is eliminated. A human private owner still has to live on the same planet as the entity he owns. Profitism eliminates that limitation.

So you end up with the government encouraged monopolies of mercantilism minus the vaguely human ownership of an East India Company:

The charter named Thomas Smythe as the first governor: 3 of the company, and 24 directors (including James Lancaster): 4 or “committees”, who made up a Court of Directors. They, in turn, reported to a Court of Proprietors, who appointed them. Ten committees reported to the Court of Directors.

It’s also eliminated some of the nationalistic element of mercantilism:

The tension was so high between the Dutch and the British East Indies Trading Companies that it escalated into at least four Anglo-Dutch Wars: 1652–1654, 1665–1667, 1672–1674 and 1780–1784.

But that element is surely there: Biden cuts China off from more Nvidia chips, expands curbs to other countries.

Okay so nailing down our current system is difficult and it’s a system still in flux. However it should be clear to everyone that profitism is very different from capitalism of old:

Robber Barons, standing for a Gilded Age of corruption, monopoly, and rampant individualism. Their corporations were the Octopus, devouring all in its path. In the twentieth century and the twenty-first they became entrepreneurs, necessary business revolutionaries, ruthlessly changing existing practices and demonstrating the protean nature of American capitalism.

Rampant individualism is over because individualism is over. We might want to continue to put a human face like Steve Jobs (died 2011) to Apple but that’s simply not the case:

Profitism is about what happens after you get your pseudo monopoly — not how you got your initial advantage. In the former corporate life cycle, profitism starts where capitalism ends and in the new life cycle there is no startup ever.
