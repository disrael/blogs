We can argue that without our diversity America would be much, much worse.

Think of an empire with very little diversity, say 19th century Britain:

Charles Booth’s monumental study of London (published starting in 1889) revealed that 30.7% of the city’s population lived in abject poverty. A decade later, Seebohm Rowntree’s study of York (1899) found almost identical numbers, with 27.8% of the population living below the basic poverty line.
The New Poor Law of 1834 centralized relief into the dreaded “workhouse” system. It operated on the principle of “less eligibility” — meaning conditions inside the workhouse had to be intentionally worse than the lowest standard of independent labor outside of it. Families were strictly segregated upon entry, meaning husbands, wives, and children were separated. By the mid-to-late 19th century, roughly 2.5% to 4.5% of the British population was receiving some form of poor relief in any given year, with hundreds of thousands confined to these penal-like institutions.
The disparity in health between the classes was staggering. According to the 1842 Chadwick Report on sanitary conditions, the average age of death for a “gentleman” in the rural district of Rutland was 52. For a laborer in the industrial slums of Manchester, the average age of death was 17 years (a statistic heavily driven by massive infant mortality rates in the slums, where upwards of 25% of children died before age five).

There is every indication that America’s diversity, far from holding us back politically, saved us from the unrestrained cruelty that so called “conservatives” push towards.

But that doesn’t tell us anything about what American society would look like without black people and other ethnic groups.

FDR’s administration was groundbreaking because it included an informal but highly influential advisory group known as the Federal Council of Negro Affairs, widely dubbed the “Black Cabinet.”
It was led by the brilliant educator and activist Mary McLeod Bethune, alongside figures like Robert C. Weaver (who later became the first Black cabinet secretary under LBJ).
This group consisted of dozens of Black professionals working across various executive departments. They fiercely lobbied the President and First Lady Eleanor Roosevelt (who was a staunch ally) to ensure New Deal relief programs, like the Works Progress Administration (WPA) and the National Youth Administration (NYA), actually reached Black communities.

What about claims that as France and Germany became less white they also became more stingy? Simply not true — resistance to immigration is not the same as attempting to withdraw social programs from existing non “white” citizens.

While the programs themselves remain popular, the growing diversity has fueled a different political phenomenon known as “welfare chauvinism.” Right-wing populist parties in both countries — such as the National Rally (RN) in France and the Alternative for Germany (AfD) — do not typically campaign on slashing the welfare state. Instead, they campaign on “national preference.” They argue that robust, generous social programs must be protected and maintained, but that access to them should be strictly limited, delayed, or outright denied to immigrants and non-citizens.
